# Grasp Execution Without a Planner :Configuration-Space Grasp Distance Fields with Certified Safety & Guaranteed Quality 

Clinton Enwerem 1, John S. Baras 1, and Calin Belta 1

Abstract —Standard multifingered grasp execution archi-tectures plan a collision-free trajectory to a selected grasp pose and track it with a feedback law. Execution-time object pose uncertainty or perturbations may invalidate the planned trajectory, forcing a costly replanning step. We present Grasp Distance Fields (GDFs), a smooth softmin distance field over grasp configurations in the arm-hand configuration space. Our controller executes a grasp by following this field’s negative gradient with a stationary feedback law, eliminating the need for a planner, stored trajectory, or grasp selection. For safety, we filter this command through a CBF-CLF quadratic program (QP) constraining self-collision, workspace, object, and obstacle clearance, reporting impeded progress as explicit slack. We prove that the softmin tracks the true set distance within 

log N/ρ for N candidates and smoothing parameter ρ, and that the filtered loop renders the safe set forward invariant. Since no smooth field captures hand-object contact switching, we switch modes with hysteresis at a pregrasp configuration, trading object collision avoidance for contact admission. A wrench-quality barrier then keeps the realized grasp’s force-closure margin within a prescribed tolerance of its value at hold onset. We evaluate in kinematic simulation on a fixed-base arm and a Unitree G1 humanoid, both fitted with the same underactuated hand. Our controller navigates cluttered and dynamic scenes to safely reach for, grasp, and lift 46 of 50 objects spanning primitive, household, and adversarial classes. Across those lifts, the executed grasps retain a median 94% of their synthesized quality margin, at 0.09 ms QP solve time per 20 ms control step. Per-step softmax weights confirm that our controller executes the nearest candidate, obviating a separate selection step. Project page: www.clintonenwerem.com/gdf .

Index Terms —Safety-critical control, dexterous grasping, control barrier functions, reactive execution, grasp quality metrics. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The execution stage of multifingered grasping remains dominated by a plan-then-track architecture. A grasp gen-erator proposes candidate grasps [ 1], [ 2 ], a selection step chooses a single grasp, and a motion planner produces a collision-free trajectory to the selected pose, which a tracking controller then follows through approach, closure, and lift [ 3]. However, under execution-time object pose uncertainty or output perturbations, the planned trajectory becomes invalid, forcing a replanning step. Such replanning may often be computationally expensive and may introduce latency that increases the reaction time of the full system, motivating closed-loop and reactive grasping [4], [5].

By contrast, we remove the replanning step entirely and represent the grasp execution target as a set of grasp configurations. We then define a smooth distance field over this set in the configuration space of the coupled arm-hand system, and specify a stationary feedback law on the resulting field. No discrete selection step remains, since the negative gradient points toward whichever candidate lies nearest in joint space from the current configuration. A control barrier function quadratic program, solved pointwise, enforces safety. The loop involves no prediction horizon, and the controller reacts to a moved object or a disturbed state by evaluating the same map [ 7 ]. Fig. 2 depicts the three obstacle settings this paper evaluates under that single law, and Fig. 1 contrasts the filtered closed loop against the nominal command on a cluttered tabletop scene, where the nominal command drives the wrist into an obstacle and the filtered closed loop avoids it. 

A. Related Work 

Reactive execution along a potential gradient appeared before most of the grasping literature. Standard operational-space potential field methods place an attractive potential at the goal and repulsive potentials on obstacles, evaluated at each control step [ 8]. Their failure mode, a local minimum arXiv:2608.00600v1 [cs.RO] 1 Aug 2026 2

away from the goal, has motivated modifications ever since. The control barrier formulation retains the reactive character while converting obstacle avoidance from a penalty into a constraint with a forward-invariance guarantee. A comparison of the two shows that every potential field induces a barrier function while the converse fails. The barrier form therefore strictly generalizes the potential field [9], [7]. Beyond reactive navigation, barrier methods extend to grasping and manipulation. The authors of [ 10 ] apply them to grasp tracking through controllers that certify no slip and no roll-off, and [ 11 ] enforces contact-force and closure constraints from measured contacts during the grasp itself. On robotic manipulators, the same filters operate at every control step in clutter, replanning around dense dynamic obstacles in a cooking cell [ 5 ]. Perception-built safety functions remove the hand-designed distance model entirely, solving a Poisson problem on occupancy data so that the barrier value comes from measured geometry [ 12 ], [ 13 ], with predictive humanoid implementations showing the construction at whole-body scale [ 14 ]. Since a multifingered grasp is not a point, extending such field-based methods to grasping requires the definition of a grasp target. By contrast, grasp fields provide the missing target without addressing execution. Neural grasp distance fields learn the distance from a query pose to the manifold of valid grasps for an object, interpret it as a cost, and minimize it inside a trajectory optimizer jointly with smoothness and collision terms [ 15 ]. Such fields are defined in task space, which requires solving inverse kinematics at execution time. Configuration-space distance fields resolve this for obstacle geometry, demonstrating that a distance representation in joint space admits one-step gradient projection where task-space signed distance fields fail on the nonlinearity of the kinematics [ 16 ]. Our field extends this construction from obstacle boundaries to a discrete set of grasp configurations, and our controller evaluates the field at every control step, whereas the prior work embeds it in an optimizer. Beyond such fields, candidate grasp sets are widely available. Grasp generators produce grasp poses or full hand configurations for grippers and dexterous hands alike, through analytical [ 3 ], [ 17 ] and generative [ 1], [ 2], [ 18 ]approaches. The generated grasps satisfy some desired quality metric, from deterministic force closure [ 19 ], [ 20 ]to robust quality measures [ 21 ], [ 17 ], [ 6] that define analytic bounds on the probability of force closure under uncertain friction and object normals. Given such a grasp set, our controller operates directly on the individual grasps through a tuple of the object-relative hand-root pose and the corresponding hand configuration (a grasp descriptor ,Definition III.1). Grasp execution software, by contrast, offers planned reaches only. Closed-loop grasping exists as a learned capability inside individual systems [ 4], and end-to-end policies embed execution in their network weights [ 22 ], [ 23 ]. Our grasp distance fields therefore provide the element these method families lack, a grasp-set target that a reactive controller evaluates pointwise. 

B. Contributions 

Specifically, we make five contributions. i. Grasp Distance Field and Smooth Approximation . We introduce the notion of a grasp distance field defined over a discrete grasp candidate set in configuration space and establish its two-sided distance bound, smoothness, and bounded-gradient properties in a form that any candidate source can instantiate. The softmax weights of the resulting field encode the candidate selection as a continuous combination, which eliminates the discrete selection step (Section IV). ii. A Certified Safe Execution Law . We give a nominal control along the field’s negative gradient, filtered through a combined control Lyapunov and control barrier function quadratic program. Its barrier con-straints jointly cover self-collision, workspace, object, and obstacle clearance. Its Lyapunov constraints record blocked progress toward the grasp in an explicit slack variable, and its equality block encodes the dependent-joint constraints of an underactuated hand. We prove that the resulting closed loop renders the safe set forward invariant, and our wrench-quality constraint holds the risk-adjusted force-closure certificate of [ 6]within a prescribed tolerance from the start of the hold (Section V). iii. A Contact-Mode Switch with Hysteresis . We give the mode structure that takes execution through contact, the single event a smooth field cannot express, stated as a hybrid system with per-mode constraint sets (Section V-B). iv. A Wrench-Certificate Bridge . We restrict admission to grasps whose risk-adjusted force-closure margin [ 6 ]exceeds a threshold and evaluate the executed grasp under the same margin, connecting the joint-space field to the wrench-space certificates of the grasping literature and reporting the min-weight margin as a baseline (Section V-C). v. Full-Description Verification . We verify the controller numerically on a 7-degree-of-freedom arm with an 11-joint multifingered hand, including a 50-object grasp descriptor evaluation, a moving-obstacle case on the same constraints, and a humanoid G1 instantiation that runs the controller without modification. Recorded per-step softmax weights over a five-candidate set give our first direct evidence for the no-selection claim, since the executed candidate is the candidate the field places nearest rather than the choice of a separate selection step (Section VI).

A. Robotic Platforms & Task Setup 

We instantiate our controller on a fixed-base arm-hand system, a 7-degree-of-freedom arm with an 11-joint multifin-gered hand and tactile sensors at the fingertips, modeled in Pinocchio from the expanded robot description, with na = 7 

and nh = 11 . One thumb joint follows another at ratio 1.83, and each outer finger joint follows the joint before it at 0.89. Five such kinematically coupled pairs appear in (10) through Am, obtained from the description at startup. In practice, exact mesh distance over the 134 self-collision pairs costs 330 ms per query, and our controller therefore replaces every collision mesh with its convex hull at load time, bringing the query to 0.1 ms . The substitution is conservative, since a hull contains its mesh. The quadratic program solves in 0.09 ms per step over 18 variables and roughly two hundred constraints, and the full step including all collision distances costs 2 ms against the 20 ms control step. Table III collects every parameter. In addition, an admission test eliminates any candidate whose pregrasp or closure violates a barrier constraint, ob-stacle constraints included, which implements the restriction of Proposition V.2. In Section VII, we measure the trapped equilibrium that results from disabling this test. Synthesis and execution share that value. D. Dynamic Obstacles 

Our controller updates the obstacle poses at every step, and therefore handles a moving obstacle without modifica-tion. We place a 5 × 5 × 45 cm obstacle at the end of the approach path and translate it at 0.05 m /s across that path during the reach. Our filtered closed loop avoids the moving obstacle and completes, entering CLOSE at step 159 against 41 without the obstacle, and completing the 12 cm rise at step 428. The filtered palm path lies up to 19 .1 cm from the nominal path, and 14 .9 cm measured point to path. The filtered closed loop thus moves around the moving obstacle rather than stopping until it passes. Surface clearance stays positive throughout, with a minimum of +8 .4 mm .In addition, we use the same case to measure the effect of the time-derivative term (10) does not include. The constraint has no ∂h/∂t contribution, and for a moving obstacle the barrier therefore falls below zero by at most the approach speed divided by the barrier rate, v/α 0 = 10 mm here. The measured minimum is −6.6 mm against the 1.5 cm obstacle margin. However, in one of the placements we tested, the closed loop reproduces the trapped equilibrium of Section VII. The obstacle reaches the approach line before the palm arrives, the filtered closed loop stops at a zero-velocity equilibrium behind it, and that equilibrium remains after the obstacle comes to rest.

We define the tabletop half-space in the pelvis frame and load a grasp record synthesized for this platform through the construction of Definition III.1. A. Trapped Equilibria of the Filtered Closed Loop 

Our construction inherits the classical limitation of artificial potential fields. The field (6) has an undesired local minimum whenever the constraints of (10) block every direction of decrease away from the candidate set and leave the projected gradient with no tangential component, the same failure potential fields exhibit in nonconvex clutter [ 8 ], and the blocked-approach scene of Fig. 2(c) pictures the resulting stop. The set-valued target mitigates this failure without eliminating it. Many candidates enlarge the region of attraction, and the admission test eliminates the candidates an obstacle blocks. When no remaining candidate admits a collision-free path, neither the larger candidate set nor the admission test creates such a path. Indeed, we test that claim directly by disabling admission on the five-candidate set of Section VI-F . Without the admission test, all five candidates remain in the target set, the closed-loop trajectory is trapped at a field value of 0.144 

just above the 0.12 guard threshold with the joint velocity decaying to 10 −14 , and with the admission test in place again, the blocked candidate drops out and the controller enters CLOSE at step 82. Admission is a static test of the pregrasp and closure configurations, and this demonstration exposes its residual gap. With the box obstacle next to the approach path, four of five candidates satisfy admission, yet the controller still stops in CLOSE without a third contact, neither completing the grasp nor contacting an obstacle. We map the boundary of this trapped region with a scene-placement search. A 50 cm column square on the approach path leaves the closed loop trapped in every trial, the controller never completes when the overlap between the obstacle and the region the palm passes through exceeds 

7 cm , always completes at 3 cm , and the boundary moves with about 2 mm of obstacle placement. In the reported scene, the trajectory passes around a first obstacle before reaching the obstacle on its line, and avoids the trapped region. A sufficiently large surrounding obstacle still leaves the closed loop trapped, and the classical modifications apply, a tangential term on the nominal or a reset on the perception estimate, both admissible without losing Theorem V.1 [9]. 

B. Limitations 

Our field depends on candidates synthesized for the current object pose, a moving object requires the candidate generator to update G at every perception step, and on hardware a pose estimate replaces ground truth, where measurement-robust constraints incorporate bounded state error into the barrier margin at a known cost in conservatism [43 ]. The verification reported here is kinematic, the executed grasps hold a quasi-static closure certificate while the lift moves an attached object, and we do not test on hardware. The tactile contact source of Section V-B defines the interface such trials would use, filters of the same form already operate at every control step on dynamic legged platforms [ 44 ], [ 14 ], [ 45 ], and the open question under dynamics concerns the wrench certificate alone. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

We introduced grasp distance fields and a certified execution law for safe multifingered grasping. Our softmin distance field over the pregrasp configurations provides a region of attraction in place of discrete grasp selection, and our CBF quadratic program enforces safety at every control step. A hysteresis-based mode structure admits contact without a replanning step, and our wrench-quality constraint holds the risk-adjusted force-closure margin within a prescribed tolerance through the lift. On an 18-degree-of-freedom arm-hand system with an underactuated hand, our controller reads every hand-specific coupling from the robot description and enforces each as an equality. The full control step, collision distances included, costs 2 ms against the 20 ms control period, and the same controller runs on a Unitree G1 humanoid arm-hand chain without modification.
